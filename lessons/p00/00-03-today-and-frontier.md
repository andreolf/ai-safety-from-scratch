## The hook

Open the news on any given day and you can find two opposite stories about AI. One says AI is overhyped autocomplete. Another says it could be the most dangerous technology ever built. Both are written by smart people. How can a beginner make sense of that?

This lesson gives you a calm map. It is the only lesson in the course that looks far ahead, and it tries hard to be fair to both sides.

## The one idea

**Some AI harms are already happening and documented. Others are possible but uncertain.** Both deserve attention, for different reasons, and serious experts disagree about how big the second group is.

Left: harms with documented cases. Right: risks the 2026 International AI Safety Report treats as emerging and more uncertain.

## A neutral referee

When experts disagree, it helps to find the most neutral summary available. In this course that is the **International AI Safety Report**. Its 2026 edition was written by over 100 independent experts, chaired by Yoshua Bengio, with nominees from more than 30 countries and organisations such as the EU, OECD and UN. It focuses on the most capable “general-purpose” AI and on “emerging risks”, which it describes like this:

:::source url="https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/2026-report-executive-summary" by="International AI Safety Report 2026, Executive Summary" date="3 February 2026" checked="26 Oct 2026"
Some of these risks are already materialising, with documented harms; others remain more uncertain but could be severe if they materialise.
:::end

## Happening now

The report lists harms that are documented today. A few examples, all in its own words or close to them:

- **Scams and fake content.** AI systems “are being misused to generate content for scams, fraud, blackmail, and non-consensual intimate imagery.” (Phase 03 of this course.)
- **Wrong answers.** Current systems “sometimes exhibit failures such as fabricating information, producing flawed code, and giving misleading advice.” (Phase 02.)
- **Over-trust.** Early evidence suggests that relying on AI tools can encourage “automation bias”, trusting AI output without enough scrutiny. (This whole course.)

Notice the honesty in the report: even for scams, it says “systematic data on their prevalence and severity remains limited.” Documented does not always mean measured.

## More uncertain, possibly severe

The report also covers risks at the frontier of capability:

- **Cyberattacks.** AI systems “can discover software vulnerabilities and write malicious code.”
- **Biological and chemical misuse.** In 2025, several developers added extra safeguards to new models “after they could not exclude the possibility” that the models could help novices with such weapons.
- **Loss of control.** Scenarios where AI systems “operate outside of anyone’s control.” The report is careful: “Current systems lack the capabilities to pose such risks, but they are improving in relevant areas such as autonomous operation.” It also notes models increasingly “distinguish between test settings and real-world deployment”, which could hide dangerous abilities from testers.

## Why experts disagree

Nobody can measure a risk that has not happened yet. The report calls this the **evidence dilemma**: AI improves quickly, but evidence about its risks is slow to arrive. Act too early and you may lock in rules that do not work; wait for proof and you may be too late. On the future pace of AI, the report says progress to 2030 “could slow or plateau”, “continue at current rates, or accelerate dramatically.” Even the experts writing it do not claim to know which.

That is why this course does not ask you to pick a side. It asks you to do two things: protect yourself from today’s harms, and read claims about the future carefully (next lesson).

:::intern alarmed
The intern watched one dramatic video about AI and has not slept since. The next day they watched one video saying it’s all hype and decided nothing matters.
---
One video is not the evidence. Look for summaries that say what is known, what is uncertain, and who says so.
:::end

:::exercise Sort the risks
Write each item in the right column, “happening now” or “more uncertain”: a chatbot inventing a refund policy; an AI that escapes its developers’ control; a cloned voice asking a grandparent for money; AI helping a novice build a biological weapon; people trusting AI answers without checking.
:::solution
**Happening now:** the invented refund policy (a tribunal case in Lesson 02.02), the cloned voice (Lesson 03.02), people trusting AI without checking (the report’s “automation bias”).
**More uncertain:** loss of control, and novice bioweapon help. The report treats both as serious enough to study and guard against, while saying current systems lack the capability for loss-of-control scenarios.
:::end
