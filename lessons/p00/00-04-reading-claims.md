## The hook

A screenshot goes viral: “New study: AI is now smarter than doctors!” It has a chart, a logo and thousands of shares. Should you believe it?

You do not need to be an expert to check. You need four questions and two minutes.

:::intern fooled
The intern shared the screenshot with the whole office before lunch. By the afternoon someone found the real study: one small test, on one kind of question, with a much more careful conclusion.
---
Shares are not sources. Open the original before you pass it on.
:::end

## The one idea

**Judge a claim by its source, its date and its evidence, not by how confident it sounds.** AI writes confidently. So do headlines.

:::figure claim-check
Tap through the four questions. They also work for claims that an AI chatbot makes to you.
:::end

## Question 1: who said it?

Every source has a point of view. That does not make it wrong, but it tells you what to look for.

- **A company about its own product.** Useful, sometimes very honest, but it is also marketing. Example: OpenAI’s own post on hallucinations admits that its models still “confidently” give wrong answers, which is to its credit. You still read it knowing who wrote it.
- **A court or tribunal decision.** Facts were tested by both sides. Lesson 02.02 uses one.
- **A government warning.** For example the FBI’s alerts on AI scams (Phase 03). Practical and cautious.
- **A research paper.** Check whether it was a big study or a small test, and whether others repeated it.
- **A review of the evidence** such as the International AI Safety Report, written by many experts from many countries.

## Question 2: when?

AI changes fast. A claim from two years ago about “what AI can’t do” may already be wrong, and a claim about “what AI will soon do” may never come true. The International AI Safety Report itself warns that evidence about AI risks “is slow to emerge and difficult to assess.”

This course shows a “checked” date next to sources for the same reason. Sources are re-checked every quarter.

## Question 3: what is the evidence?

Ask what the claim rests on.

- A number needs a source. “Up to 34.7% errors” is checkable (Lesson 02.04). “AI is 90% accurate” with no source is not.
- One example is not a trend. One amazing demo, or one scary failure, tells you something is *possible*, not how *often* it happens.
- Watch the words. “Could”, “may” and “up to” are doing a lot of work in many headlines.

## Question 4: can I open it?

The last test is the simplest. Can you click through to the original and find the sentence that supports the claim? If the link is missing, broken, or the page says something different, treat the claim as unverified.

This matters even more with chatbots, which can invent sources that look real. Lesson 02.06 practises this. And on this site, anything that could not be confirmed is marked <span class="flag">NEEDS SOURCED DATA</span> instead of being stated as fact.

:::callout key A two-minute habit
Before you believe or share a claim about AI: who said it, when, what is the evidence, and can I open it? If you cannot answer the last one, do not share it yet.
:::end

:::exercise Check a real claim
Open OpenAI’s post “Why language models hallucinate” (linked in Sources). Answer the four questions: who wrote it, when, what evidence it gives, and whether you can find the table it describes.
:::solution
**Who:** OpenAI, a company writing about its own models (and those of others). **When:** 5 September 2025. **Evidence:** it points to a research paper and shows a table from the GPT-5 system card comparing two models on the SimpleQA test. **Can I open it?** Yes: the table lists abstention, accuracy and error rates, for example an error rate of 26% for gpt-5-thinking-mini and 75% for OpenAI o4-mini. A good source, read with the knowledge that a company is describing its own work.
:::end

## Quick check

:::quiz
- q: A headline says “AI could replace all teachers by next year”, with no link. What is the best first step?
  options: ["Look for the original source and check who said it, when, and on what evidence", "Share it so others are warned", "Believe it because it sounds confident"]
  answer: 0
  explain: Confidence is not evidence. Find the source; “could” and a missing link are both warning signs.
- q: Why does this course show a “checked” date next to sources?
  options: ["Because AI changes fast and old claims can go out of date", "Because old sources are always wrong", "For decoration"]
  answer: 0
  explain: Dates let you judge whether a claim may be outdated. Sources here are re-checked every quarter.
- q: A company publishes a post about its own AI model. How should you read it?
  options: ["As potentially useful, while remembering who wrote it and why", "As automatically false", "As a neutral scientific review"]
  answer: 0
  explain: Company posts can be honest and informative, but they are not neutral. Read them knowing the author.
:::end
