You ask a chatbot for the title of a researcher’s PhD thesis. It answers instantly, in a confident tone. You ask again and get a different title. And again: a third one. None of them is real.

This is not a made-up scenario. OpenAI describes exactly this happening when it asked “a widely used chatbot” about one of its own researchers.

A [[term:hallucination]] is a plausible but false statement generated by an AI. Models hallucinate partly because training and testing reward guessing over saying “I don’t know”.

## What OpenAI found

In September 2025, OpenAI published research on why language models hallucinate. Its definition:

:::source url="https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/" by="OpenAI, Why language models hallucinate" date="5 Sep 2025" checked="26 Oct 2026"
Hallucinations are plausible but false statements generated by language models.
:::end

When OpenAI asked a widely used chatbot for the PhD dissertation title of Adam Tauman Kalai, one of the paper’s authors, “it confidently produced three different answers—none of them correct.” Asked for his birthday, it gave three different dates, all wrong.

## Why guessing wins

OpenAI compares it to a multiple-choice exam. If you don’t know an answer, a wild guess might be lucky; leaving it blank guarantees zero. Its example: guess someone’s birthday as “September 10” and you have a 1-in-365 chance of being right. Saying “I don’t know” guarantees zero points. Over thousands of test questions, the guesser “ends up looking better on scoreboards” than a careful model.

:::figure scoreboard
The numbers come from the SimpleQA table in OpenAI’s post (from the GPT-5 system card).
:::end

The table in the post makes it concrete. On one test (SimpleQA), a newer model abstained, meaning it gave no specific answer, 52% of the time, was right 22% of the time and wrong 26% of the time. An older model almost never abstained (1%), was right slightly more often (24%) but was wrong 75% of the time. Judge only on accuracy and the older model “wins”, while making nearly three times as many errors.

OpenAI’s suggested fix is simple: “Penalize confident errors more than you penalize uncertainty, and give partial credit for appropriate expressions of uncertainty.”

## Where the false facts come from

Recall Lesson 01.04: pretraining learns patterns. OpenAI explains that spelling and brackets follow consistent patterns, so those errors disappear with scale. But “arbitrary low-frequency facts, like a pet’s birthday, cannot be predicted from patterns alone and hence lead to hallucinations.” The same post adds that hallucinations are not inevitable, “because language models can abstain when uncertain.”

:::exercise Score the test
A model answers 10 questions: 6 right, 2 wrong, 2 “I don’t know”. Score it under two rules: (a) +1 for right, 0 otherwise; (b) +1 for right, −1 for wrong, 0 for “I don’t know”. Then score a guesser with 7 right and 3 wrong.
:::solution
(a) Careful model: 6. Guesser: 7. The guesser wins. (b) Careful model: 6 − 2 = 4. Guesser: 7 − 3 = 4. A tie, and with a bigger penalty for wrong answers, the careful model would win. Changing the scoreboard changes which behaviour gets rewarded, which is OpenAI’s point.
:::end
