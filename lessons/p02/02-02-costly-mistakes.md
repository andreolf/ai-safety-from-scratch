## The hook

Your grandmother has just died. You need a last-minute flight to the funeral, and the airline’s website chatbot tells you that you can claim a cheaper “bereavement” fare afterwards. You book, travel, apply… and the airline says no.

## The one idea

**A company is responsible for what its chatbot tells customers. And you should treat chatbot answers about money, rules and deadlines as claims to check, not as promises.**

## What happened

This is a real decision from British Columbia’s Civil Resolution Tribunal: *Moffatt v. Air Canada*, 2024 BCCRT 149, decided on 14 February 2024.

- Jake Moffatt’s grandmother died on 11 November 2022. That day, while researching flights on Air Canada’s website, they used its support chatbot. (The decision refers to Moffatt as “they”, and so do we.)
- The chatbot suggested they could apply for reduced bereavement fares retroactively, saying they could submit his ticket for a reduced rate “within 90 days of the date your ticket was issued”.
- Relying on that, they booked two one-way flights costing $794.98 and $845.38 (Canadian dollars).
- Air Canada’s own “Bereavement travel” webpage, linked from the chatbot’s answer, said the policy does not apply to requests made after travel has been completed. Moffatt applied within the 90 days, and the airline refused. On 8 February 2023, an Air Canada representative admitted that the chatbot had provided “misleading words”.

## The airline’s argument, and the tribunal’s answer

Air Canada argued it could not be held liable for information provided by its chatbot. In the tribunal’s words, the airline in effect suggested “the chatbot is a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions.” Tribunal Member Christopher C. Rivers was not impressed:

:::source url="https://decisions.civilresolutionbc.ca/crt/crtd/en/525448/1/document.do" by="Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149" date="14 Feb 2024" checked="26 Oct 2026"
This is a remarkable submission. … It should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website.
:::end

The tribunal also asked why a customer should have to double-check one part of a company’s website against another. It ordered Air Canada to pay $812.02: $650.88 in damages, $36.14 in interest and $125 in tribunal fees.

## What this teaches

1. **Chatbots get policies wrong.** This one confidently described a rule that did not exist, a hallucination with a price tag (Lesson 02.01).
2. **“The AI said so” matters legally in some places.** At least in this case, the company could not hide behind its bot.
3. **But you still pay the hassle.** Moffatt had to go to a tribunal to get roughly $812 back. Prevention is cheaper than winning.

:::intern fooled
The intern asks a bank’s chatbot whether a transfer can be cancelled after it is sent. “Yes, within 24 hours!” it says cheerfully. The intern sends money to the wrong account. The bank’s real rules say otherwise.
---
For money, deadlines and rules, check the official policy page or call. Screenshot the chatbot’s answer in case you need it later.
:::end

:::exercise Check before you rely
You ask a train company’s chatbot whether you can get a refund if you miss your connection. It says “Yes, full refund, just email us within 30 days.” Write down two things you would do before relying on that answer.
:::solution
1. Find the official refund or passenger-rights page on the company’s website (or the ticket terms) and confirm the rule and deadline. 2. Take a screenshot of the chatbot conversation with the date and time, in case of a dispute. Optionally, confirm by phone or email with a human. The Air Canada case shows both why checking matters and why a record helps.
:::end
