## The hook

Imagine a face-analysis system that is right almost every time for some people, and wrong about one time in three for others. Would you call it “accurate”? On average it might look fine. For the people it fails, it is not.

## The one idea

**AI learns patterns from data. If the data under-represents some groups, or carries past unfairness, the AI can work worse for those groups. An average accuracy number can hide this.** This is what people mean by AI [[term:bias]].

## Gender Shades

In 2018, researchers Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru published *Gender Shades*. They tested three commercial gender classification systems, software that looks at a photo of a face and guesses whether the person is a man or a woman.

First they looked at the data. Two well-known face benchmarks were “overwhelmingly composed of lighter-skinned subjects”: 79.6% for one (IJB-A) and 86.2% for the other (Adience). So they built a new test set balanced by gender and skin type, using a skin-type scale used by dermatologists. Then they measured each group separately.

:::source url="https://proceedings.mlr.press/v81/buolamwini18a.html" by="Buolamwini & Gebru, Gender Shades (Proceedings of Machine Learning Research, vol. 81)" date="2018" checked="26 Oct 2026"
darker-skinned females are the most misclassified group (with error rates of up to 34.7%). The maximum error rate for lighter-skinned males is 0.8%.
:::end

## Why this matters for chatbots too

Gender Shades studied face analysis, not chatbots, but the lesson carries over. Large language models learn from huge amounts of text (Lesson 01.04). Some languages, regions and communities are much less represented in that text than others, and the text reflects the views and stereotypes of the people who wrote it. You saw one practical effect in Lesson 01.02: less common languages are cut into more tokens. Projects like Swiss Apertus, with 40% non-English training data, are one response (Lesson 01.07).

## What you can do

- **Ask “accurate for whom?”** when you see a single accuracy number.
- **Notice when an answer assumes** a gender, culture or country you did not mention.
- **Be extra careful with decisions about people**, such as screening job applications, credit checks for loans or school admissions. The EU AI Act lists all three as high-risk uses in its Annex III (points 4(a), 5(b) and 3(a)); see lesson 09.02.

:::intern alarmed
The intern uses an AI tool to sort job applications. It quietly ranks applicants from one region lower, because past hires mostly came from somewhere else. Nobody checked the results by group.
---
When AI makes or shapes decisions about people, check its results group by group, and keep a human responsible.
:::end

:::exercise Read the average
A system reports “95% accuracy”. In the test, 90 people are from group A and 10 from group B. It is right for 88 of group A and 7 of group B. What is its accuracy for each group? What would you ask the seller?
:::solution
Overall: (88 + 7) / 100 = 95%. Group A: 88 / 90 ≈ 98%. Group B: 7 / 10 = 70%. The headline hides that group B is wrong three times in ten. Ask: “What is the accuracy for each group, how many people from each group were in your test, and does that match the people who will use this?”
:::end

  answer: 0
  explain: The study measured each group separately, which revealed a large gap hidden by average accuracy.

  options: ["They were overwhelmingly made up of lighter-skinned subjects", "They had too many photos", "They used only cartoons"]
  answer: 0
  explain: 79.6% and 86.2% lighter-skinned subjects, so the tests themselves could hide failures.

  answer: 0
