You have now seen chatbots invent facts (02.01), describe rules wrongly (02.02), flatter (02.03), fail unevenly (02.04) and forget things backwards (02.05). Does that mean you should never use them? No. It means you need a habit for checking.

Use AI to draft and explore, then check anything that matters at the source. The real danger is “automation bias”: trusting AI output without enough scrutiny.

## Automation bias

The International AI Safety Report 2026, written by a large international group of experts, puts it plainly:

:::source url="https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/2026-report-executive-summary" by="International AI Safety Report 2026, Executive Summary" date="3 Feb 2026" checked="26 Oct 2026"
Early evidence suggests that reliance on AI tools can weaken critical thinking skills and encourage ‘automation bias’, the tendency to trust AI system outputs without sufficient scrutiny.
:::end

The same report lists the reliability problems you have met in this phase: current AI systems “sometimes exhibit failures such as fabricating information, producing flawed code, and giving misleading advice.” It also says capabilities are “jagged”: leading systems “may excel at some difficult tasks while failing at other, simpler ones.” So a model that impressed you on a hard question can still trip on an easy one.

## The four-step check

:::figure check-flow
Step through the routine. It takes a minute for most questions.
:::end

1. **Ask for the source.** “Where does this come from?” A link or a title is a lead, not proof.
2. **Open it yourself.** AI can produce links and titles that look real but do not exist, or that say something different.
3. **Find the exact sentence.** Check the number, the date and who said it. Close is not the same.
4. **High stakes? Ask a qualified human.** For health, legal and money decisions, use AI to prepare your questions, not to make the decision.

## How much checking?

Match the effort to the stakes. A dinner recipe idea needs little. A medicine dose, a legal deadline, a payment or anything you will publish under your name needs the full routine. Asking the same chatbot “Are you sure?” does not count as a check: a sycophantic model may simply agree with whatever you seem to want (Lesson 02.03).

:::intern proud
The intern asks a chatbot for the deadline to appeal a parking fine. Instead of trusting the answer, the intern asks for the source, opens the city’s official page and finds the real deadline, which is 10 days shorter.
---
One minute of checking at the source beats a confident wrong answer. That is what the four steps are for.
:::end

:::exercise Run the routine
A chatbot tells you: “In Switzerland, you must report a lost passport to the police within 48 hours.” Describe how you would check this using the four steps.
:::solution
1. Ask the chatbot for its source. 2. Do not rely on its link alone: go to an official site yourself, such as the federal government’s page on identity documents or your canton’s police site. 3. Look for the exact rule: is there really a 48-hour limit, or just an instruction to report the loss promptly? 4. If it matters (for example, you are travelling soon), call the passport office or police. The point is to trust the official source, not the AI’s wording.
:::end
