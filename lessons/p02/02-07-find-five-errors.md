Mistakes in AI answers rarely look like mistakes. They come in the same calm, fluent tone as the true parts. The only way to get good at spotting them is practice.

Hunt for five kinds of mistakes: an invented number, a wrong date or name, a fake citation, an out-of-date fact, and advice that is too sure for the stakes.

## The answer to check

Below is the kind of paragraph a chatbot might give if you asked it to “summarise what we know about chatbot mistakes”. We wrote it for this exercise. Every sentence sounds reasonable. Five of them contain a problem.

> **AI answer:** (1) Chatbots make mistakes because they learn patterns from text and predict likely next words. (2) In the Gender Shades study, the worst error rate for darker-skinned women was 3.47%. (3) In December 2023, OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update because it had become too flattering. (4) As Miller and Ortiz showed in *The Journal of Honest Machines* (2022), chatbots that apologise are 80% more accurate. (5) Today’s chatbots can only read about 2,048 tokens at a time. (6) In *Moffatt v. Air Canada*, a tribunal held the airline responsible for what its chatbot said. (7) So if a company chatbot tells you a refund rule, you can safely act on it without checking.

## Hunt

Read each numbered sentence and decide: true, or which kind of mistake? Use what you learned in Phases 01 and 02. Then open the exercise below.

:::exercise Find the five errors
Which five sentences are wrong, and what kind of mistake is each one?
:::solution
- **(2) Invented number.** Gender Shades reported error rates of up to **34.7%**, not 3.47%. A misplaced decimal point changes the story completely (Lesson 02.04).
- **(3) Wrong date.** OpenAI rolled back the sycophantic GPT-4o update in **April 2025** (post dated 29 April 2025), not December 2023 (Lesson 02.03).
- **(4) Fake citation.** This paper and journal were invented for this exercise. Real-looking references are a classic hallucination: always look them up (Lesson 02.01).
- **(5) Out-of-date fact.** 2,048 tokens was GPT-3’s training context size, according to 3Blue1Brown. Modern models have much larger windows (Lesson 01.06).
- **(7) Too-sure advice.** The Air Canada case shows chatbots *do* get rules wrong. The customer won, but only after going to a tribunal. Check the official policy first (Lesson 02.02).

Sentences (1) and (6) are fair summaries of Lessons 01.03 and 02.02.
:::end

## Why this works

Notice how the mistakes hide: a decimal point, a plausible date, a confident-sounding journal, a fact that *was* true once, and a conclusion that goes one step too far. None of them “looks wrong”. That is why the checking routine from Lesson 02.06 starts with the source, not with a feeling.

:::intern proud
The intern now keeps a sticky note on the screen: number, date, citation, still true?, too sure? Every AI answer that matters gets a quick pass with the note before it goes into a report.
---
A short checklist turns vague suspicion into a habit.
:::end

## Quick check

:::quiz
- q: In the practice answer, what was wrong with the Gender Shades number?
  options: ["It was 34.7%, not 3.47%", "The study never measured error rates", "It was about chatbots"]
  answer: 0
  explain: A small slip in a number can make a serious failure look minor. Check numbers at the source.
- q: Why is a fake citation especially dangerous?
  options: ["It looks like evidence, so readers may not check it", "It is always obviously wrong", "Citations are never used by AI"]
  answer: 0
  explain: Invented references look just like real ones. Look them up before relying on them.
- q: The sentence “Today’s chatbots can only read about 2,048 tokens” is which kind of error?
  options: ["An out-of-date fact", "An invented citation", "Correct"]
  answer: 0
  explain: 2,048 tokens was GPT-3’s context size. A fact can be true once and stale later.
:::end
