## The hook

A few years ago, faking a convincing photo, a voice or a video took skill and time. Today, apps can do it in minutes. Most people use them for fun or work. Criminals use them too.

## The one idea

**[[term:generative-ai|Generative AI]] creates new text, images, audio and video. The FBI warns that criminals use all four to make scams faster, larger and more believable.**

:::figure genai-four
The four kinds of fake content in the FBI’s December 2024 warning.
:::end

## What the FBI says

In December 2024, the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) published a public service announcement titled *Criminals Use Generative Artificial Intelligence to Facilitate Financial Fraud*:

:::source url="https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203" by="FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center, Alert I-120324-PSA" date="3 Dec 2024" checked="26 Oct 2026"
The FBI is warning the public that criminals exploit generative artificial intelligence (AI) to commit fraud on a larger scale which increases the believability of their schemes.
:::end

The FBI adds that generative AI “reduces the time and effort criminals must expend to deceive their targets”, and can “correct for human errors that might otherwise serve as warning signs of fraud”. It is also careful to say that creating synthetic content “is not inherently illegal”; the problem is using it for crimes such as fraud and extortion.

## The four kinds, in the FBI’s examples

- **Text:** fake social media profiles in bulk, messages written faster for more targets, translations that “limit grammatical or spelling errors”, and chatbots embedded in fake websites.
- **Images:** realistic profile photos, fake ID documents, images of celebrities promoting counterfeit products, and pictures of disasters to collect donations for fake charities.
- **Audio (“vocal cloning”):** short clips of “a loved one’s voice to impersonate a close relative in a crisis situation”, and cloned voices used to get into bank accounts.
- **Video:** fake “real time video chats with alleged company executives, law enforcement, or other authority figures”, and fake promotional videos for investment fraud.

The rest of this phase takes the most common of these one at a time: voice clones (03.02), deepfake video calls (03.03), AI-written phishing (03.04) and fake celebrity ads (03.05). Then Lesson 03.06 gives you the one habit that works against all of them.

:::intern fooled
The intern sees a photo of a flooded village shared with a link to “donate now”. It is moving and looks completely real. The charity does not exist, and neither did that exact scene.
---
Realistic is no longer the same as real. Give through charities you already know, using their official website.
:::end

:::exercise Sort the scam
For each, name the kind of generative AI the FBI would put it under (text, image, audio or video): (a) a call from your “grandson” in tears; (b) a perfectly written email from your “bank”; (c) a Teams call with your “boss” asking for a transfer; (d) an ad showing a famous presenter holding a miracle product.
:::solution
(a) Audio (vocal cloning). (b) Text (AI-written, no spelling mistakes). (c) Video (real-time video chat with an alleged executive). (d) Image, or video if it moves (celebrity promoting a product). Real scams often combine several, as the Arup case in Lesson 03.03 shows.
:::end
