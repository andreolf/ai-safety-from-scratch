## The hook

You get an email from head office about a secret transaction. It feels off. Then you join a video call, and there they are: the chief financial officer and several colleagues you recognise, looking and sounding like themselves. Your doubts fade. You make the transfers.

## The one idea

**A [[term:deepfake]] can put familiar faces and voices on a live video call. Seeing someone is no longer proof it is them. For money and secrets, the rule is: verify through a separate, known channel.**

:::end

## What happened at Arup

In May 2024 CNN reported that Arup, the London-based engineering firm behind landmarks such as the Sydney Opera House, confirmed it had been the target of a deepfake scam in Hong Kong. A spokesperson confirmed “that fake voices and images were used.”

According to Hong Kong police, as reported by CNN:

- A finance worker first suspected he had received a phishing email from the company’s UK office, because it “specified the need for a secret transaction”.
- He was then invited to a video call with people he believed were “the chief financial officer and other members of staff, but all of whom turned out to be deepfake re-creations.”
- He put his doubts aside because the people on the call “looked and sounded just like colleagues he recognized.”
- He sent 200 million Hong Kong dollars, about US$25.6 million, across 15 transactions (as reported by public broadcaster RTHK, citing police).

Arup’s global chief information officer, Rob Greig, said the number and sophistication of attacks, including deepfakes, “has been rising sharply in recent months.”

## The lesson: the red flag was there first

Notice the order. The worker’s instinct was right: a secret transaction requested by email *is* a red flag. The deepfake call was used to overcome that doubt. The FBI lists this exact tactic: videos “for real time video chats with alleged company executives, law enforcement, or other authority figures.”

What would have stopped it is not better eyesight. It is a process:

- **Call back on a known number** (from the company directory, not the email) before moving money.
- **Two-person approval** for large or unusual payments.
- **Treat “secret” and “urgent” as reasons to slow down**, not speed up.

## Not only for companies

The same trick is used on individuals: fake “bank managers”, “police officers” or celebrities on video. The FBI also warns about videos used in private chats to “prove” that an online contact is a “real person”.

:::intern alarmed
The intern’s “manager” appears on a video call, camera slightly blurry, asking for gift cards for a client “before the meeting, no time to explain”. The intern starts to buy them, then remembers the rule and calls the manager’s desk phone. The real manager is on holiday.
---
A face on a screen is not a signature. For money, verify on a separate channel you already trust.
:::end

:::exercise Design the rule
You run a small business with three employees. Write a two-line payment rule that would have stopped the Arup scam.
:::solution
Example: “Any payment over CHF 1,000, or any payment described as urgent or secret, needs a call-back to the requester on a number from our own contact list, and a second person’s approval.” “No exceptions for video calls, emails or messages, whoever they appear to come from.” The rule works because it does not depend on spotting the fake.
:::end
