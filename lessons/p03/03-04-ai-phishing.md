## The hook

For years the advice was: “Look for spelling mistakes.” A scam email from your “bank” full of odd grammar was easy to bin. Now the same email can arrive in flawless German, French or Italian, addressed to you by name.

## The one idea

**[[term:phishing|Phishing]] messages written with AI no longer look sloppy. Judge a message by what it asks you to do (click, log in, pay, share a code), not by how well it is written.**

:::figure phish-then-now
The same scam, before and after AI writing tools. The request is identical.
:::end

## What changed

The FBI’s December 2024 warning lists several ways criminals use AI-generated text:

:::source url="https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203" by="FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center, Alert I-120324-PSA" date="3 Dec 2024" checked="26 Oct 2026"
Criminal use generative AI tools to assist with language translations to limit grammatical or spelling errors for foreign criminal actors targeting US victims.
:::end

(The small grammar slip, “Criminal use”, is in the FBI’s original text.) The same alert says criminals “create messages to send to victims faster allowing them to reach a wider audience with believable content”, use AI for “spear phishing” (messages tailored to a specific person), and even “embed AI-powered chatbots in fraudulent websites to prompt victims to click on malicious links.”

So two old warning signs are weaker: bad language and generic “Dear customer” greetings.

## What still gives it away

AI can polish the words. It cannot change what the scammer needs from you. Look at the **request**:

- **A link to log in** or “confirm your details”.
- **Urgency or a threat:** account blocked, parcel returned, fine due today.
- **A payment or a code:** pay a small fee, read back an SMS code, approve something in your banking app.
- **A sender or link address that doesn’t match** the real organisation (press and hold, or hover, to see it without clicking).

The safest habit does not depend on spotting anything: **don’t use the link in the message.** Open the official app or a saved bookmark and check there.

:::intern fooled
The intern gets a perfectly written email from “IT Support” with the company logo, the intern’s name and a link to “re-confirm your password before Friday”. No typos, so it must be real. The link leads to a lookalike login page.
---
Good grammar is not a trust signal any more. Judge the request, and go to the real site yourself.
:::end

:::exercise Ignore the polish
An email reads: “Dear Ms Keller, as part of our new security process, please verify your account within 24 hours using the secure link below to avoid suspension. Kind regards, Your Bank.” It is perfectly written. List three warning signs and the safe action.
:::solution
Warning signs: urgency (24 hours), a threat (suspension), and a request to log in through a link in the email. Using your name proves nothing: names are easy to find. Safe action: don’t click. Open your bank’s official app or type the address yourself, or call the number on your card. If there is a real problem, you will see it there.
:::end
