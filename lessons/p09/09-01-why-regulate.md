## The hook

A company uses an AI system to sort job applications. You are rejected in seconds. You ask why. Nobody can tell you, not even the company, because the system can’t explain itself. Is that allowed?

## The one idea

**Governments regulate AI for the same reasons they regulate cars and medicines: to protect people’s safety and rights, and to build enough trust that people will use the technology. The hard part is doing it without blocking the benefits, and the world has chosen different balances.**

## The European argument

The European Commission explains why it proposed the EU AI Act:

:::source url="https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai" by="European Commission, AI Act" date="" checked="26 Oct 2026"
For example, it is often not possible to find out why an AI system has made a decision or prediction and taken a particular action. So, it may become difficult to assess whether someone has been unfairly disadvantaged, such as in a hiring decision or in an application for a public benefit scheme.
:::end

It adds that “although existing legislation provides some protection, it is insufficient to address the specific challenges AI systems may bring.” It is also careful to say that “most AI systems pose limited to no risk and can contribute to solving many societal challenges.” The rules are aimed at the uses that can hurt people.

You have met these problems in this course: unfair errors (lesson 02.04), the black box (lesson 08.01) and the evaluation gap (lesson 08.03).

## Rules that depend on the use

The approach Europe chose is called risk-based: the rules depend on what the AI is used for, not on the technology itself. A spam filter and a CV-sorting system might use similar techniques, but only one decides whether you get a job. Lesson 09.02 shows the four tiers.

## Different balances around the world

- **The EU** passed a binding law, the AI Act, which the Commission calls “the first-ever comprehensive legal framework on AI worldwide.”
- **The Council of Europe**, a separate organisation of 46 member states, wrote a treaty on AI and human rights (lesson 09.04).
- **Switzerland** decided to adapt existing laws sector by sector rather than copy the EU (lesson 09.05).
- **The United States** has a voluntary risk management framework from NIST, a government standards agency (lesson 09.06).

Each approach weighs the same things differently: protecting people, keeping innovation, and trust.

:::intern neutral
The intern says: “Regulation is pointless, AI moves too fast for laws.” Then the intern’s own job application is rejected by an automated system with no explanation and no one to appeal to.
---
Rules are how you get a right to an explanation, a human to talk to, and someone responsible when things go wrong.
:::end

:::exercise Where would you draw the line?
List three uses of AI you think should need strict rules, and three that need none. For each, write one sentence on why.
:::solution
Answers vary. A common pattern: strict rules for uses that decide things about people’s lives (hiring, loans, school places, policing, medical decisions), and none for uses where mistakes are cheap and easy to notice (spam filters, game opponents, photo filters). That is close to the EU’s risk-based logic in lesson 09.02.
:::end
